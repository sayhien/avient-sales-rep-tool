Notes. Why a large language model? Here is the business case, with numbers that I checked. I say where a number is only my estimate. Without a model, developers must build many parts: speech to text, handwriting reading, word rules for each field, message templates and texts for the reasons. I found no public figure for this build, so the time is my estimate: 6 to 9 months for 3 developers. The rules also keep growing after the launch, with each new word, language and habit of the reps. With a model, one connection does the language work. Guides from two software companies give 6 to 10 weeks for one feature, or 4 to 8 weeks for a simple connection, plus 2 to 3 weeks to plan. For several features, I use 2 to 3 months for 2 developers, with tests on real notes. For money, a mid level backend developer in Singapore earns about 7 to 9 thousand Singapore dollars a month. A salary guide of March 2026 says so. I use 8 thousand. The employer adds 17 percent for the pension fund. So one developer month costs about 9.4 thousand dollars. Three developers for 6 to 9 months cost about 170 to 250 thousand. Two developers for 2 to 3 months cost about 40 to 55 thousand. The usage fee is small. For 10 reps with 10 notes a day, the list price of the Kimi K2.6 model gives about 30 Singapore dollars a month. A price list of August 2026 shows 0.75 US dollars for a million input tokens and 3.50 for output. Other sources show lower prices. The data row matters most. For the first version, Hue uses the own service of Moonshot, the company behind Kimi. Its terms say that Moonshot may use content to improve its models. To stop this, Avient must sign an enterprise agreement. Moonshot keeps data on servers in Singapore, but it does not say how long. So in the first version, Avient should use sample data only, until the agreement is signed. Later, if Hue grows to other teams, Avient can run an open model on its own servers. Then the data stays inside Avient. But the model is very large, so the hardware costs much more. That needs a new business case. Speech to text may stay a separate service, and I did not price it. The technology team of Avient must check all these numbers. Sources: the salary guide of SkillUp, the pension rules of Singapore, the price list of CostBench, the terms and the privacy policy of Moonshot, and the guides of RaftLabs and SFAI Labs.

Waiting

Waiting has three parts. A promise due today: 50 points, because a broken promise costs trust. The customer waits for Daniel: 10 points each day. Daniel waits for the customer: 5 points each day after the usual wait. His own delay counts double.

Daniel does not see the points. He sees the reason in words. These numbers are my first values. Real sales reps must test them.

Notes. How does Hue know which follow up is first? It uses a rule. Points are the deal value times the chance of an order, plus the waiting time. Waiting has three parts. A promise that is due today gets 50 points, because a broken promise costs trust. This is as much as a large deal. When the customer waits for Daniel, each day gets 10 points. When Daniel waits for the customer, each day after the usual wait gets 5 points. His own delay counts double, because he can end it today. Each of these two stops at 30 points, so a small old item does not stay at the top. Here is an example. The price for Jasmine has 139 points, so it is first. A late payment has its own rule, because money that a customer owes comes before most new deals. Daniel does not see the points. He sees the reason. These numbers are my first values. A test with real sales reps must confirm them.
