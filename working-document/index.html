Research, ideas and decisions

Avient Sales Assistant: Research and Define

Loo Say Hien · Started 30 September 2026 · Updated 5 October 2026

Try the prototypeOpen the presentation

Daniel is a sales rep at Avient Specialty Inks (Avient's own title for the job is technical sales manager). He visits print shops across Singapore, and he needs a tool that helps him keep track of his deals without writing up notes at night.

This document is a record of the work, in the order that it happened. The early parts use the words of that time, such as "tasks", "quiet deals" and "tidy up". The fourteenth review, near the end, lists the words that the tool uses now.

Scope: one company, one person

The brief is for all business to business sales. I made it smaller: one company (Avient Specialty Inks), one role (sales rep) and one job (keep track of deals with the least effort). The brief says "sales rep" and Avient's job description says "technical sales manager". We use "sales rep" so the deck matches the brief, and mention the real title once.

Why narrow it down

  • Every industry has different deals and buyers. That is too much for a first version.
  • Avient gives real limits. It sells through distributors, and it offers technical help and papers that prove its inks meet safety rules.

In the first version

  • A spoken briefing: a plan for the whole week on Monday, then the top three things each day, with a way to see more
  • Quick ways to capture a meeting (a few words, a tag, a voice note or a photo of his notebook) and a short spoken debrief afterwards
  • Warnings when a customer goes quiet, and a list of old quotes worth another try
  • Email and missed calls added automatically, and WhatsApp chats shared by Daniel
  • A simple view of how far he is from his yearly target

Not in the first version

  • Other industries or other Avient businesses
  • Quotes, prices and orders
  • Social media posting tools

Main persona: Daniel Lim, Sales Rep

Daniel knows textile printing inks very well, but he is new to selling. He wants to win by finding new customers and by replying faster than anyone else.

"I know the ink better than anyone. What I forget is who I owe a reply, and who has gone quiet."

Snapshot

  • Age 31. Lives in Singapore. 8 years in textile screen printing, the last 2 as a sales rep at Avient Specialty Inks.
  • Looks after his own area: about 40 customers and 30 open deals, sold directly and through distributors.
  • Out visiting print shops most days, in places like Ubi, Kaki Bukit, Woodlands and Tuas. He works mostly from his phone.
  • Talks to customers on WhatsApp, by phone and by email, in English, Mandarin and sometimes Malay.
  • Has a yearly sales and profit target, and old quotes that never turned into orders.

How he thinks about the job

These ideas come from the videos you watched. They are guesses to test.

  • Experienced reps rely on old relationships. He wins by being first and most helpful.
  • A fast reply makes him look professional.
  • He works backwards from his yearly target.
  • He counts small wins to stay motivated.

Goals

  • Reach his yearly sales and profit target
  • Know what to do first each day
  • Be trusted by customers as an expert, not just someone who quotes prices

Pain points

  • He writes up visits at night from memory, so details are lost.
  • He cannot tell which customers are waiting for a reply.
  • A customer who has gone quiet looks the same as one who is still testing the ink (a slow trial).
  • Old quotes sit forgotten in email and files.
  • His notes are spread across chats, a notebook and his own spreadsheet.

Behaviours

  • Talks more than he types: calls, voice notes and quick chat replies
  • Replies to hot leads at once from his phone, then forgets to record what he said
  • Plans his day by area, not by deal
  • Wants a nudge, not a lecture

What he will not accept

  • Long forms
  • Typing the same thing twice
  • Reminders about deals he knows are dead

A typical day

  1. 8:00 In the car: he wants to know who is waiting and who has gone quiet.
  2. 10:00 At a print shop: he runs a trial and hears a price objection.
  3. 12:30 At a coffee shop: he replies to a distributor and tries to remember what was said.
  4. 15:00 At a second shop: he wants to see the history of an old quote before he speaks.
  5. 21:00 At home: he writes it all up from memory. The tool should make this step disappear.

What success looks like

Daniel opens the tool in the car. It talks to him and tells him the top three things for the day. After meetings he just tells it what happened. There is nothing to write up at night.

The two worst moments are stage 3, where information is lost within hours, and stage 5, where nothing is visible.

What we learned

Five findings from the job description, the videos and our own thinking. The last one is a guess to test.

  1. The best information comes up in the meeting, but there is no easy and polite way to keep it. Typing on a phone looks like he is not listening. Reps in an online forum say a pocket notebook works best for trust, but it is slow to type up later. Voice notes help, but they pile up on his phone and nobody turns them into a record.
  2. Fast replies win deals, but he must know who is waiting. One sales advice video says very fast replies make a rep stand out.
  3. Old and quiet customers are the easiest sales to win back, but nobody can see them. Two videos suggest looking again at old quotes and lost customers.
  4. Selling is personal and a team effort. Customers buy from people they trust, so the record should also hold what Daniel knows about the person, such as who decides and what they care about. Priya, Ryan and Mei Ling need the same facts, so nobody asks the customer the same question twice.
  5. Customers may not accept being recorded. Reps in a forum thread say they record only with permission, and that handwritten notes build trust. Whether Singapore print shop owners agree is a guess to test.

Problem statement

Daniel needs to know four things at any time: how far he is from his target, which deals matter most, which deals have gone quiet, and what to do next. Today the facts are spread across his head, WhatsApp, email, a notebook and voice notes, so he cannot see them together. Keeping them up to date means writing notes at night, which he skips or does from memory, so deals go quiet without him noticing.

In one line: Daniel's deal information is scattered across his phone and his head, and updating it takes time he does not have. So he cannot see which deals matter, and deals go quiet without him noticing.

Does it match the brief? The brief lists four goals. The problem statement covers each one.

The brief asks for In the problem statement How the tool answers it
Check sales progress "how far he is from his target" A simple view of the yearly target
Prioritise high value deals "which deals matter most". Value is not only deal size. It also depends on how likely the deal is and how soon it could close. The Monday plan and the top three, with a reason for each
Spot stalled deals "which deals have gone quiet" Warnings that depend on the stage of the deal
Take fast action without tedious notes "what to do next", and no writing at night One tap actions, voice and quick capture

How might we

  • How might we let Daniel pass on what he learns by talking, without breaking his conversation with the customer?
  • How might we turn scattered notes, chats and old quotes into a clear picture of each customer?
  • How might we tell Daniel which deals matter most today, in a few seconds?
  • How might we make quiet deals easy to see without nagging him?
  • How might we show Daniel how far he is from his target, and what would move it, without a report to fill in?

What the tool could do (first ideas)

In this idea, the tool is a light customer record system (often called a CRM) with an assistant on top. It keeps a record for each customer and each deal. It works on his phone first, and also on a tablet and a computer, all showing the same records. Customers do not use it. They keep using WhatsApp, calls and email.

The tool should feel like a helper, not a chore. It talks, asks very little, and never forces Daniel to type. Priya, Ryan and Mei Ling are not designed in this version. Later, Priya could get a simple view, Ryan a status message, and Mei Ling a one page brief.

Creating and editing a record

  • The tool starts a record from the first email, call or a photo of a business card, and Daniel confirms it.
  • It fills the record in from what it hears and reads. Each change shows as a suggestion that Daniel accepts or fixes in one tap.
  • Daniel can edit by voice ("Jasmine's new press manager is Alan") or by tap. Bigger clean ups are done on the computer.
  • Before it makes a second record, it asks "Is this the same shop?"

Home dashboard. One screen that sums up everything: full on the computer and tablet, short on the phone. The spoken briefing reads out the same facts.

  • How far he is from his yearly target
  • Deals by stage, with the total value in each
  • The top three for today
  • Customers waiting for his reply, and customers gone quiet
  • Promises due this week
  • Recent wins and losses

Edge cases

  • Weak signal: it must work offline and update later.
  • Quiet is not always a problem: a slow trial means the customer is still testing the ink on real jobs, which can take weeks, so silence is normal. Busy seasons such as National Day orders and year end also slow replies. So the tool should not warn after the same number of days for every deal. It looks at the stage of the deal. Example numbers to test: trial running, wait 3 weeks; quote sent, nudge after 10 days; customer asked a question and Daniel has not answered, warn the same day.
  • Two sellers, one customer: Avient sells directly and through distributors, so Daniel and a distributor's rep may both contact the same print shop. The customer then gets two calls or two prices, and the distributor may be upset. Before Daniel contacts a customer, or revives an old quote, the tool shows who last spoke to that shop and whether a distributor passed on a lead or has an order. Daniel can then talk to the distributor before he contacts the shop, so the customer hears one voice and one price.
  • Too many alerts: keep them few, and let him dismiss one in a tap.
  • Wrong guesses: chats mix English, Mandarin and Singlish. He can fix a mistake in one tap. Some reps may prefer Mandarin or Malay. This is noted and not built yet.
  • Privacy: customer details are personal data under Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act. Do not record customers without permission.
  • Shared screens: never show another customer's data on a tablet, and never share prices with distributors.

Ideas and how to choose

These are the ideas so far. Nothing is chosen yet. The scores below are our first guesses and need testing with reps.

How to choose

  1. Score each idea from 1 to 5 on three things. Impact: how much it helps Daniel and the brief's four goals. Effort: how hard it is to build (5 is hardest). Privacy risk: how much it intrudes on Daniel or a customer (5 is worst).
  2. Treat privacy as a gate, not a trade. Impact and effort can be traded against each other, so they go on a matrix. Privacy risk is different. A risky idea can break the law or the customer's trust, and no amount of impact makes up for that. Any idea with a privacy risk of 4 or 5 is parked until we find a safer version, for example asking permission or recording only Daniel's voice.
  3. Put the ideas that pass on an impact against effort matrix. High impact and low effort come first.
  4. From those, pick a small core that together covers the brief's four goals, and prototype it.
  5. Check the privacy scores with a print shop owner and two reps, because they are our guesses.

Rules

  • A number earns its place only if it changes what Daniel does next.
  • Rank the top three by deal value, chance to close and days waiting, and always say why.
  • Every number shows its change since the last period, with an arrow and words, not only colour.
  • The phone shows level 1, the tablet levels 1 and 2, the computer all three.

Should every customer have their own dashboard? No. Each customer gets a record page with a header of four facts: stage, last contact, next step and open quote value. A dashboard per customer only makes sense for a few Key customers, later.

Design rules so far

  • Listen to Daniel, not to customers
  • Talk like a helpful colleague: short and two way
  • Daniel talks, the tool writes it up, Daniel confirms
  • Three things a day, each with a reason. Monday is the exception: a plan for the whole week
  • Capture is one small floating button that opens a short menu. Hold it to talk at once.
  • The menu has the same four ways on a phone and a tablet: Talk, Type, Write and Photo of a notebook page. A computer has Type, Talk and Attach a photo.
  • The look is soft, light and wide. It uses a softened Avient blue and the grey of the Avient wordmark, with no green. Status is always a word and an icon.

Notes for the deck

Add one technical page. One interviewer is technical, so the deck needs a page that explains the hard technology in plain words. It should cover:

  • Voice capture. How speech becomes text, how it copes with accents, mixed languages and a noisy van, and what happens with no signal.
  • Writing with a pen. How handwriting on a phone or a tablet becomes text.
  • Photo of a notebook page. How the tool reads text from a photo of a page.
  • Automatic tidy up. How a language model pulls out the customer, the next step and the date, how it shows what it is unsure about, and why Daniel always checks before saving.
  • Quiet deal warnings. The simple rule behind them (days since last contact against what is normal for that stage), so it is clear this is not a black box.
  • Privacy and storage. Voice of the sales rep only, consent before recording a customer, and where notes are kept.
  • Phone, tablet and computer. What is shared, what differs between Android and iPhone, and what an engineer still needs to confirm.

Prototype devices. The clickable prototype covers phone, tablet and computer. The computer is for office work: more on one screen and a proper keyboard.

Decisions from the first prototype review

  • On the phone, Brief and Dashboard become one screen. The sales bar opens to show the dashboard detail. The tabs are Brief, Records and Settings.
  • The "Gone quiet" list is removed. Quiet deals and late payments sit together under "Needs attention", and each row names the customer straight away.
  • The assistant has a name and a mark, so Daniel can see what it wrote or picked: the top three, the week plan, drafted messages, tidied notes, suggestions and warnings.
  • A typed note can be saved as it is. Tidy up is a choice, not a step he must take.
  • The due date uses a date menu.
  • The customer record has section links that stay at the top, and each section has its own Add button. "Notes and files" is now "Files".
  • Edit shows only the fields that can be changed.
  • Default text is larger, and Settings lets him change it.

Decisions from the second prototype review

  • One Brief on every device. Phone, tablet and computer have the same three places: Brief, Records and Settings. The Brief has the same sections in the same order: sales, top three, this week, needs attention and open deals. Bigger screens show the sections side by side. This replaces the earlier idea of a separate Dashboard on bigger screens.
  • Section links on the Brief. Top three, This week, Attention and Open deals stay at the top, like in the customer record.

Decisions from the twelfth review

  • Menus and dates use our own control. The control of the browser has a different look on each device, and it did not match the tool. Now each menu and each date field is one control in the style of the tool. The value and the icon have the same space from the left edge and the right edge: 12 pixels.

Decisions from the fourteenth review

This review changed many words. The reason is the same each time: a sales rep must understand a word with no training.

  • "Tasks" are now "Follow ups". A follow up is a thing to do for a customer that is not a visit: a call, a message, a quote or a question about a payment. "Task" was too general.
  • "Quiet" is now "No reply". The label says "No reply for 16 days". "Quiet" needed an explanation. "No reply" does not.
  • "Not spoken in 2 weeks" is now "Not in touch". The filter has a second row: 2 weeks, 3 weeks, or 4 weeks or more. In the record, "Last spoke" is now "Last contact".
  • "Tidy up" is gone. The tool now says what Hue does: Hue reads the note and fills in the fields. The buttons are "Check now" and "Check later".
  • Money has no "S". "$612k", not "S$612k". All amounts are in Singapore dollars, and the Guide says so. A rep in Singapore does not need the letter.
  • The same label in each place. A status has the same words on Home, in Records and in the record.
  • The count of follow ups includes the hidden ones. "0 of 27 done" counts the 24 below "Show 24 more". A count of only the three on the screen was not honest.
  • A late payment is a follow up. It has its own rule for the order: 40 points, plus the late amount, plus one point for each day that it is late. Money that a customer owes comes before most new deals.
  • A late payment comes with a plan. The sheet gives the reason, a possible cause, and a plan of payments that Daniel can offer. The plan follows the finance rules, so he needs no approval. The message goes to the accounts contact, not to the main contact.
  • Check later. After a visit he is often in a hurry. "Check later" keeps the note at the top of Home, in "Notes to check". The note goes into the record after he checks it.
  • One line at the top of Check and save. "Hue filled in 6 fields from your voice note. Check each field, then save." Before, each field had its own mark and the date had a "Please check" label. One line at the top is calmer and asks for the same check. Below a field, Hue gives the reason for its choice.
  • A guide in each note sheet. "A good note says these things": Customer, What happened, Next step, Date, Product. Hue ticks each one when it finds it. So he learns what to say while he talks.
  • Hue reads notes better. It finds products. It keeps his sentences as he said them. The timeline has no mark "Tidied by Hue", because he checked the note himself.
  • The weather panel shows the weather now. The picture moves, and the colour of the panel changes: yellow for sun, grey for rain, dark blue for night. He can slide the row of the next parts of the day.
  • The menu "Sample time" is removed from the prototype page. One menu fewer for the reviewer. "Sample weather" stays.
  • Filters in Records have a second row. "Open deals" chooses a stage. "Not in touch" chooses the number of weeks. A row of filters slides when it is too long.
  • Back goes to the row that he came from. He does not search for his place in the list again.
  • The calendar has a month and a year. A tap on the month or the year changes it. Saturday and Sunday have a grey ground.
  • The address field suggests streets. He types a part of the street, the building or the postal code.
  • The words of the automatic tick. "Done. Hue ticked it when you saved your note." Before, it said "Done by your note", which was not clear.
  • All links are on GitHub. One link page opens the film, the prototype, the presentation, the guide, the design system, this document and the deck. A reviewer needs no account.
  • The screen guide is now "How Hue Works". Each part starts with what Daniel gets. The section "Removed, and why" is gone. The table of sample follow ups shows the reason that Daniel reads.
  • The design system is now "Hue Design System". It has the new labels, the weather panel, the filters and the follow up row.
  • The deck, the presentation and the film use the new words and new pictures.

What the fourteenth review assumes

  • The accounts system can give the payment status of each customer.
  • The finance team has rules for payment plans, and a rep can offer a plan that follows them.
  • The usual wait for a reply is 3, 7, 14 and 5 days for the four stages. These are first values. A manager can change them, and Hue learns the usual wait of each customer.
